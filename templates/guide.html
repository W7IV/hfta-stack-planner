HFTA Stack Planner: getting started

One page to read before your first study. About ten minutes.

What it does

The planner answers one question: on my ground, which antenna heights, stacks and tower positions give the best signal towards the places I want to work? It runs the HFTA method (N6BV's HF Terrain Assessment, as reimplemented in the open-source OpenHFTA engine) over your real terrain, for every tower, target and band you define, and ranks the results. Every number it shows is one OpenHFTA run, or simple arithmetic on runs, and the Report page gives you the exact inputs to repeat any run in the original HFTA.

It is a planning tool for Yagis and stacks on towers, 80 m to 10 m. It is not an antenna modelling program: antennas are the generic HFTA types (dipole, 2 to 8 elements), and HFTA knows nothing about mutual coupling, feedlines or the ground to the side of the path.

Important safety and performance disclaimer

Not engineering advice, a tower-safety assessment, or a performance guarantee. This planner is free experimental software for comparing possible HF antenna arrangements. A high figure of merit, positive guy clearance, or absence of warnings does not prove that a design is safe or will work as predicted.

Do not use it to size, approve, erect, or modify towers, guy wires, foundations, anchors or antennas. The optional guy/ice model is simplified geometry; it does not analyze structural loads, wind, dynamic movement, foundation or anchor capacity, material fatigue, or all collision hazards. Independently verify plans and consult appropriate qualified professionals and manufacturers for construction and safety decisions.

HFTA uses generic antennas and simplified terrain and propagation statistics. It does not model all coupling, losses, installation details, weather, or real-time propagation, and results have only limited reference-case validation. User-supplied data and third-party services can be inaccurate or unavailable. No accuracy, performance, availability, or security is guaranteed.

The five steps

The left rail is the order of work. A worked example runs through it: a station in New Hampshire with one tower, choosing between a tribander stack and a 40 m Yagi.

1. Sites

  1. Add site, give it a name, type latitude and longitude.
  2. Fetch terrain online. After half a minute the card shows "360 profiles, every 1°" and the data source.
  3. Look at the far horizon chip. Below 1°: nothing beyond 4.4 km matters. Near or above 1°: a distant ridge; run the fetch again with the 10 km option and compare.
  4. Check "Compare terrain" at your main bearing: does the profile look like the ground you know? A profile that is flat where you have a hill means a wrong position.

Add more sites if you have more than one place for a tower. The same towers are then compared at every site.

2. Targets

  1. Add elevation-angle files: Add .PRN files for ARRL files, or Generate with VOACAP…. Choose the site as origin, check any or all standard regions (each gets its own .PRN file), and choose months, hours and solar levels. Custom receive points can still be generated separately.
  2. Add target for each region you care about, choose its file. The bearing is computed from your site; Bearings from site position recomputes all of them, and the map lets you drag a bearing or widen the spread.
  3. Tick the bands that matter for each target. Untick the rest; they cost runs and add nothing.
A spread of ±8° in 8° steps (three headings) is a sensible default. It stops one lucky azimuth from deciding the result.

3. Towers

  1. Add tower. One row per antenna: height and elements per band. A tribander is one row with elements on 20, 15 and 10 m; "Band set…" fills the common cases.
  2. Set Location to the site the tower stands on, or "Every site" to try it everywhere.
  3. Tick the targets it serves. A fixed European stack serves Europe only; a rotating tower serves everything.
  4. Optional: the guy table and radial ice, for the clearance check of turning antennas.
  5. 6 m antennas can be entered too, for the drawing, the spacing check and the guy check. They never get a FOM: HFTA stops at 30 MHz.

Stack Designer (below the tower cards) does the searching for you: give it the antenna types, how many of each, the tower height and the rules, and it finds the layout with the best score over all ticked targets. The search is exact within its height grid, and the summary says so; on slow terrain it searches a coarse grid first (a few minutes) and then fine around the best, and the progress line says how long. Cancel stops it. Use as tower puts the winner on the Towers page.

4. Compare

  1. Evaluate. The first run takes a few minutes; later runs reuse what has not changed.
  2. Pick a target button. Each row is a site and a tower; each cell is the FOM on that band, with the switch setting HFTA chose. Bold green = best in the column.
  3. Worldwide shows all targets together, for a tower that must do everything.
  4. Click any value to see the runs behind it, the elevation pattern with the arrival angles, and the exact HFTA inputs.

5. Report

Downloads: a self-contained HTML report, an Excel workbook with the runs as live formulas, CSV, and the replay pack (every terrain file, every run with its inputs and the expected pattern, for checking in HFTA itself). The default downloads are shareable: they leave the .PRN files out and list them by checksum. "With .PRN files" is for your own records.

Reading the numbers

  • FOM (dB): antenna gain at each elevation angle, weighted by how often openings arrive at that angle on that path. Higher is better.
  • Under 0.5 dB: not a real difference. About 1 dB: where it starts to matter on the air. 3 dB: half an S-unit, a clear win.
  • Overall (Designer and Worldwide): per target the mean over its bands, then the weighted mean over targets. A planning score, not an HFTA FOM.
  • A hill that falls away towards the target often makes a lower antenna win: the slope already gives low angles, and more height adds nulls at useful angles. That is the point of the tool.
  • * beside a value: a few angles of that run were filled by interpolation after the calculation core returned invalid values. failed: no result for that run. ⚠: a spacing warning.

What HFTA cannot tell you

  • Nothing above 35° elevation. HFTA computes gain from 1° to 35°, the DX angles, and the FOM counts only arrival angles in that range. It cannot score NVIS or any other high-angle path; a target whose table has most of its weight above 35° is not evaluated, and the reason says so.
  • Nothing about your specific antenna: only the generic type for its element count.
  • No mutual coupling: a stack closer than about 0.3 wavelength is rated too well. The spacing warnings mark this.
  • Perfect phasing and power split, no feedline loss, no wind.
  • One terrain slice per heading. A hill 30° off the bearing does not exist for it.
  • The guy check is a simplified geometry screen, not a safe-clearance or structural determination. Independent qualified engineering is required for design decisions.
  • Terrain data measures tree tops. Elevation-angle tables are statistics over a solar cycle, not tonight's opening.
  • The soil setting hardly matters. HFTA models horizontal antennas, and the ground reflection at low angles is almost the same for every soil: under 0.2 dB of FOM on 80 m, under 0.1 dB from 40 m up, water included. Leave it at Average. (Ground constants matter a great deal for verticals and for ground loss in NEC models; K6STI's HF ground constants page is the reference for that work.)

Good habits

  • Run Flat ground once as a reference: it shows what your terrain adds or takes away.
  • Use Copy, moved… to move a site 100 ft and see whether the result survives. If it does not, the result is a fluke of the data.
  • Keep the hosted copy's login to yourself. Give other people the shareable exports.
  • When something looks wrong, open the value: the runs behind it and the pattern explain most surprises.

Running it yourself

The planner runs on your own computer with no account and no internet, except for the online terrain fetch. The downloads page lists a Windows package (unzip and double-click), and a Docker image for macOS, Linux and Windows. Details in the README of the project.
